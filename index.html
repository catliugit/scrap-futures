The problem

Useful fabric gets left behind. The people who need it can’t find it.

Deadstock rolls, roll ends and cutting-room offcuts sit in warehouses and factories. Meanwhile designers, costume departments and makers hunt shop by shop for fabric that fits a project. Surplus is usually found by word of mouth, if at all, and recyclers say they can’t plan without predictable supply (Kearney 2026; Textile Exchange).

Today

Deadstock is hard to find, and harder to match

Good surplus fabric exists, but it’s scattered, rarely described by width, length and composition, and often gone by the time you hear about it.

Leftover rolls and offcuts take up storage, then sell for little or go to waste. Finding the right buyer for each lot takes time nobody has.

How it works

One catalogue, two ways in.

Suppliers bring fabric and buyers bring requests. Both meet in the same catalogue, and each lot goes to the best next use we can find, reuse first. During the pilot, matching is done by hand.

01

List or forecast

Suppliers list confirmed lots, or expected ones with a quantity range and a date window.

02

Describe it properly

Form (roll, roll end, panel, bundle or bulk), composition, width, usable length, colour and condition. Not just kilograms.

03

Match both ways

New lots are checked against open requests, and new requests against current and expected lots.

04

Route and record

Reuse first; recyclers take what can’t be reused. We record where each lot went.

🔒

Suppliers stay in control. Nothing is published without your sign-off, and lots can be listed without your brand name. Production data shared for forecasting stays under NDA and is deleted on request.

For suppliers

List what’s left, or what will be.

Factories, brands, fabric warehouses and workrooms can list surplus for free during the pilot. You set the terms; we look for the buyer.

01 · What to send

A short description

Fabric type and composition, colour, width, amount in metres or kg, condition, location and photos if you have them. For expected lots, a rough range and month are enough.

02 · What you control

Your terms

Sale, discount or donation. Collection or delivery. Minimum order. Whether your name appears. Nothing goes live without your sign-off.

03 · What happens next

We look for a match

We describe the lot, check it against open requests and introduce buyers by email. You agree terms and ship directly; Scrap Futures never holds stock.

How the model works, and its limits
  • Data: 8,000 production runs from a simulator that nests graded pattern pieces into cutting plans, then adds the losses cutting plans miss: selvedge trim, ply-end allowance, roll-end remnants, spreading loss and defects.
  • Model: gradient-boosted quantile regression (P10/P50/P90) on order data only, exported to JSON and run here in plain JavaScript.
  • Held-out accuracy: 8.8% mean error on offcut kg (82% of runs within ±15%), vs 23.3% for the cutting plan alone and 20.3% for a flat 15% rule. The P10–P90 range covers 76% of outcomes. Simulated waste averages 19% of fabric, inside the 10–20% industry range.
  • Limits: trained on simulated, not factory, data. The first pilot goal is ±15% on real weighed output. Grades follow a v0 standard to be co-defined with recyclers.

FAQ

Common questions.

Something not covered? Use the form below.

Is the fabric on this page real?

Not yet. The listings shown are samples that demonstrate how Scrap Futures will work. Real lots will be listed once suppliers join the pilot, and they’ll be clearly marked as confirmed or expected.

What does “expected” mean?

An expected lot is fabric a supplier thinks they’ll have, for example after a production run. It shows a quantity range and a date window rather than a fixed amount, and becomes “confirmed” once the fabric exists and has been checked.

Can I buy a couple of metres for a personal project?

Not at first. Small orders cost almost as much to cut, pack and post as large ones, so the pilot starts with project-sized lots. Bundles and local collection for individuals are planned. Post a request anyway, as it helps us learn what people need.

Who holds and ships the fabric?

The supplier. Scrap Futures doesn’t buy or store material. We describe the lot, find a match and introduce both sides; fabric goes directly from supplier to buyer.

Doesn’t cutting software already know the waste?

It knows the planned waste inside one cutting plan (marker). It doesn’t predict the weighed offcut after roll ends, remnants, spreading and defects, which in our simulated model adds about a quarter more, early enough to plan around. We aim to work alongside cutting software, not replace it.

How is this different from waste-traceability platforms?

Traceability platforms such as Reverse Resources map factory waste after it’s generated, mainly for recycling. Scrap Futures puts reuse first, lets buyers post requests, and adds expected supply before the fabric exists.

Why London?

It’s close to the first buyers: independent designers, theatres, and fashion and costume courses. It’s also within reach of UK suppliers holding surplus today. The forecasting work is aimed at factories in sourcing hubs later on.
